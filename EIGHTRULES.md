# Ben Shneiderman's Eight Golden Rules of Interface Design

Ben Shneiderman's Eight Golden Rules are a set of principles intended to help designers create interfaces that are consistent, intuitive, and easy to use. This is just a summary here for reference that I put together from my old class notes. For more information, read the original [here.](https://www.cs.umd.edu/~ben/goldenrules.html)

## 1. Strive for consistency

Maintain consistent terminology, layouts, colors, and interactions throughout an interface.
Users should be able to apply what they have learned in one part of an application to other parts without having to relearn how things work. If exceptions are needed, they should be limited in number.

**Example**: Keeping navigation menus and button styles consistent across every page of a website.

## 2. Seek universal usability

Design interfaces that accommodate people with different abilities, experience levels, and technological needs.
Consider accessibility, device differences, and varying levels of familiarity with technology.

**Example:** Providing keyboard navigation, readable text, and accessible labels for interactive elements.
 
## 3. Offer informative feedback

Provide clear and timely feedback whenever a user performs an action.
The amount of feedback should reflect the significance of the action. Minor actions may require subtle feedback, while major operations should receive more noticeable confirmation.

**Example:** Displaying a confirmation message after successfully submitting a form.

## 4. Design dialogs to yield closure

Organize interactions into sequences with recognizable beginnings, middles, and ends.
When a task is completed, provide confirmation, so users know they can move on

**Example:** Showing an order confirmation page after completing an online purchase.

## 5. Prevent errors

Design interfaces to reduce the likelihood of mistakes and provide clear, constructive guidance when errors occur.
Whenever possible, prevent invalid actions rather than relying solely on error messages afterward.

**Example:** Validating form fields before submission and explaining how to correct invalid entries.

## 6. Permit easy reversal of actions

Allow users to undo or reverse actions whenever practical.
Reversible actions encourage exploration and reduce anxiety about making mistakes.

**Example:** Providing an Undo option after deleting an item.

## 7. Keep users in control

Design interfaces that allow users to initiate and direct actions rather than feeling controlled by the system.
Experienced users especially benefit from predictable behavior and responsive controls.

**Example:** Allowing users to cancel an operation rather than forcing them to wait for it to finish

## 8. Reduce short-term memory load

Minimize the amount of information users must remember while navigating or completing tasks.
Favor visible options, recognizable elements, and accessible instructions over requiring users to recall information from memory.

**Example:**  Displaying available formatting options in a toolbar instead of requiring users to memorize commands.
